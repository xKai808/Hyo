How does that influence how you do [your] job?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> Well, I&#8217;ve had a lot of jobs, which has opened me up to a lot of different networks of people. So I&#8217;m not coming out of journalism school and writing about the DTC world, or about the hospitality world, or about newsrooms that I&#8217;ve never been in. I&#8217;ve worked in newsrooms, I&#8217;ve worked in venture-backed companies, I&#8217;ve worked at Facebook, so I can write about all these things&#8212;I don&#8217;t want to say &#8220;with authority&#8221;&#8212;but with experience. I know what the chairs feel like in the Meta offices at Hudson Yards.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> What do they feel like?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> Bouncy, like very adjustable. Everything is like, bring it up, bring it down. I know what the New York Magazine office&#8212;</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> What are those chairs like compared to Facebook&#8217;s?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> That was the only job that I had a proper cubicle at. It was at the old Varick Street office when Adam Moss was the editor. I loved that job so much. Olivia Nuzzi was one of the first women to ever come up to my desk and be like, do you want to go smoke a cigarette? That happened to me. I&#8217;m not writing about her from over here. I hung out with her. You build up your own sources. You&#8217;re not blind-calling anyone, because you&#8217;ve been around a bit. I think that has given me a lot of perspective when I write about work.</p><p>A big thing when I started Feed Me in its current form is that I like to write about the way that people spend money and what that says about them. And I think that&#8217;s changed a bit. It&#8217;s a little bit more like New York news, and it&#8217;s probably a little bit less judgy about how people literally spend money. I think I was probably forcing myself into that narrative a bit.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> You used to be more judgy?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> I didn&#8217;t have an audience. I could hit so hard. I have so many people watching me now, it&#8217;s hard. I also don&#8217;t really have a team, so if I have a bad day, there&#8217;s nobody I&#8217;m really looking at [who&#8217;s] being like, &#8220;It&#8217;s fine.&#8221;</p><p style="text-align: center;">~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> I worked at Meta, and during Covid I started writing on Substack. I started writing short horror fiction that was super informed by my time working in social media and the sort of consumer moment that was happening. Matt Levine from Bloomberg linked to one of those stories in his newsletter, and a bunch of my friends sent me photos of their terminals. They were like, &#8220;Levine put you in Money Stuff.&#8221; And I was like, wait, you&#8217;re reading newsletters for pleasure, and there&#8217;s cross-promotion in newsletters? It opened my eyes to the traffic potential but also the pleasure potential of reading newsletters. Because in the past, it was like this chore that you gave to somebody on the audience team, like &#8220;Aggregate a bunch of stuff and put it in the newsletter.&#8221; It has to happen, nobody wants to do it, but we have to send it.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> The newsletters were used to try and drive traffic to the stories of the property.</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> Right, right. To check off a box. And the best-case scenario was that somebody wouldn&#8217;t [flag] it [as] spam or unsubscribe. And I was freelance writing at the time while I was at Meta. I wrote this story about the phenomenon of &#8220;shoppy shops&#8221; for New York Magazine. I thought: tech people want more of this. Consumers want more of this kind of writing. And the CPG brands and marketers also want to be talking about this more. So I started writing shorter versions of that every day behind a paywall on Substack.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> Why did you do the paywall?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> Because I could, and I wanted a little bit of space. I didn&#8217;t have an online following. So I was just like, let me keep this safe and protected, and if you really want to read it, you can. I had really hot takes, and at the time most of my friends who were having those kinds of takes were being taken care of by an editor, to make their takes cooler or safer or maybe a bit more palatable.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> People appreciated that you didn&#8217;t have that filter. And you put it behind a paywall, so it&#8217;s kind of like a secret club.</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> Exactly. And I think that&#8217;s why my comment section... I mean, there are thousands and thousands of paid readers below that paywall now. It&#8217;s such a wonderful experience. Every day when I see my comment section, I&#8217;m never scared that anything bad is happening there.</p><p style="text-align: center;">~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> So what does it feel like to be the owner of this business and responsible for this thing that is suddenly big?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> It&#8217;s great. It&#8217;s a big business. You guys see the network effects of it all, and how many people read it. Everyone sees how big it is because of the leaderboards, which is great for me. I&#8217;m very proud that I have amassed that many people without, you know, bringing them over from somewhere else. But that part of the job is also pressure, like the purple checkmark. It doesn&#8217;t <em>not</em> put a target on your back as a journalist, because it&#8217;s so different from other people who are working in journalism who are not having the same type of experience at all.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> The purple checkmark on Substack means you&#8217;ve got at least 10,000 paying subscribers. And so a lot of journalists are probably looking at their shrinking newsrooms, the jobs disappearing, the insecurity... and potentially jealous of that, do you think?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> Yeah. People can do back-of-napkin math and figure out how much money I&#8217;m making, and I&#8217;m sort of tired of being on panels and always being asked that. And then being followed on a talk or panel by an investor or a CEO of a media company who&#8217;s never asked that. That&#8217;s something I started actually pushing back on in interviews.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> Do you think that&#8217;s because you&#8217;re a young woman running the business, or do you think it&#8217;s a media business curiosity thing?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> I think it&#8217;s both. But a smart person can also see that I&#8217;m putting money back into my writers and I&#8217;m putting money back into my free parties. I also know that I&#8217;m doing more than just writing. I&#8217;m a product manager, a social media manager, a graphic designer. I&#8217;m emailing my warehouse all the time, talking on the phone with them about stocking products. I&#8217;m running payroll. I&#8217;m an event planner, an event producer. I do a lot of jobs. And I love my job. I feel crazy half the time, which might be coming through in this conversation, but it&#8217;s an awesome job. I feel really grateful when I can connect with other media operators and trust them and get along with them. But I also feel like there&#8217;s this competitiveness, especially with people who have been doing this longer than me and might look at me like, where the fuck did you come from?</p><p style="text-align: center;">~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~^~</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> What&#8217;s cool is that a lot of the people that write for me aren&#8217;t career writers. <span class="mention-wrap" data-attrs="{&quot;name&quot;:&quot;J Lee&quot;,&quot;id&quot;:64915371,&quot;type&quot;:&quot;user&quot;,&quot;url&quot;:null,&quot;photo_url&quot;:&quot;https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8a0be816-368d-4b9f-b668-f84d8b3f19f2_510x510.jpeg&quot;,&quot;uuid&quot;:&quot;be08374d-3279-4222-aa5b-391ee385f54c&quot;}" data-component-name="MentionToDOM"></span>, who hosts Expense Account, the Feed Me podcast about restaurants&#8212;he&#8217;s an artist, he was a wine distributor. And I was like, I kind of want to go to Sundance, but I&#8217;m not going to go because I have this SF trip. And I was like, yeah, put together a budget, tell me what it would look like. Yeah, go. We can run this. Nobody else is writing about it. Get in there. Be weird. I love identifying interesting people and then doubling down on them and having them build out their own little worlds within the newsletter. But it&#8217;s also real&#8212;I&#8217;ve never managed anybody before, so it&#8217;s new for me and I&#8217;m learning as I&#8217;m doing it. The only thing I can do is talk to other media operators who have been through it and try to get best practices from them. There are some true business benefits to running a newsletter on Substack&#8212;and this isn&#8217;t an ad&#8212;but a lot of the operations are sort of dealt with, and the founders and the other people that work at the company are so present on the feed that you can reach out to them and get a response.</p><p><strong>Hamish:</strong> I hear they&#8217;re real pricks.</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> No, they&#8217;re great. People are feeling a lot of doom and gloom at the moment, but do you have any feelings of optimism?</p><p><strong>Emily:</strong> Here&#8217;s what I&#8217;m wary of: taking cash up front for a business that doesn&#8217;t exist yet.
