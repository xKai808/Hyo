They met at Trees Lounge inside the Nitehawk Cinema in Prospect Heights, Brooklyn, one of Harris&#8217;s home theaters, sipped tequila gimlets, and filled in the rest.</span></p><div><hr></div><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> So you have subscription revenue as your core, but you also have a podcast, you do TV writing, you still do some freelance for legacy media, and you do some brand stuff. How do you think about the whole mixed-revenue stream, the business of Hung Up?</span></p><p><strong><span>Hunter:</span></strong><span> The newsletter is my job. Everything else feels like something fun, something I feel curious about. But I do feel very seriously that people who read the newsletter are customers, and I am fulfilling my contract with them. I just think it&#8217;s the most disrespectful thing in the world to be like, &#8220;Okay, maybe it&#8217;ll come out, maybe it won&#8217;t.&#8221; Before I owe anyone else work, I owe them work&#8212;the readers. And then the brand stuff is just kind of fun. Sometimes it&#8217;s like working a different muscle. Like I&#8217;ll edit a TikTok and want to kill myself, because my thumbs are too big and my nails are too long to open CapCut. The podcast started as: I just want to have something with my best friend. And it all kind of feels like an ouroboros, where I&#8217;m talking about the same thing again and again. But there are people who read the newsletter but don&#8217;t listen to the podcast, and vice versa. And the TV stuff is maybe my dream. But if I could write my own series tomorrow, I would still keep the newsletter, because in the middle of the night I&#8217;m like, &#8220;I love a girl&#8217;s emotional support inner wrist tattoo,&#8221; and I need a place to say that.</span></p><div><hr></div><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> You&#8217;ve described yourself as having insane tunnel vision. That when you get one of these obsessions, you can really, really follow it. Why do you think that works for you and your audience?</span></p><p><strong><span>Hunter:</span></strong><span> I think we just both have the same disease: being insatiable. I think that&#8217;s it. Part of it is growing up as an only child, where I just had to entertain myself a lot. And so I maybe will spend more time with something than other people did. The tunnel vision thing really came from one of my old editors who was like, &#8220;You have really bad tunnel vision.&#8221;</span></p><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> Was it a compliment or an insult?</span></p><p><strong><span>Hunter:</span></strong><span> No, it was an insult. She meant it. But then I was like, &#8220;Oh, yeah, I totally do.&#8221; In every part of my life.</span></p><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> What&#8217;s the most embarrassing obsession that you&#8217;ve ever had tunnel vision about?</span></p><p><strong><span>Hunter:</span></strong><span> Charlie Puth. I went through a big Charlie Puth phase. He really was the pop prince. Like, he should have been Justin Bieber of the next generation. And then there was one time that I went to see him in concert at Radio City, and I was singing along to every single song and did not mute my camera. And so everyone heard me singing along. One of my friends still talks about it to this day.</span></p><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> How do you think about how much of yourself to bring into a celebrity profile?</span></p><p><strong><span>Hunter:</span></strong><span> I think, none. In an ideal world you can get a sense of my curiosities, but it&#8217;s not about me. Whenever I read a profile that feels like too much about the writer, I&#8217;m like, okay, but what did George Clooney have to say? </span></p><p><span>And then there&#8217;s a real romance when you&#8217;re preparing for one, where you read everything about this person, everything they&#8217;ve ever said, and then you go into the interview and they are disappointing, just because you&#8217;ve built them up in your head. But that disappointment should not show up in the piece. There&#8217;s a real moment where you have to become very dispassionate. It&#8217;s almost like a breakup, because you really have to break up with your idea of someone and just tell the story as it happened.</span></p><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> Is the disappointment because you&#8217;ve added something into the picture? Or is it always the same, that they&#8217;re more human than you thought?</span></p><p><strong><span>Hunter:</span></strong><span> I think it&#8217;s just that they are human in general. It&#8217;s hard to get to know someone like Julia Roberts and be like, &#8220;Oh my god, she&#8217;s real. She&#8217;s a real person.&#8221; It&#8217;s honestly like when you go out on a date with someone. A little bit of air has just left, because now you&#8217;re real in front of me. You know when someone exists on a page for so long and then it happens, and it&#8217;s like: okay, we&#8217;re just two people talking.</span></p><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> A lot of your writing in Hung Up weaves in your personal experience and relationships. At first I thought I wanted my friends to contribute and all of that. And then pretty quickly I was like, no. This is mine. I&#8217;m just such a perfectionist, I have to have my eyes on every single thing. I&#8217;ve grown to love that about the newsletter. I&#8217;m a star student, star employee, but a terrible boss. And I&#8217;m the only person deciding everything, which is nice.</span></p><div><hr></div><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> What&#8217;s your writing process actually like? I have a note in my phone of ideas that I think about when I wake up or when I go to sleep. Day to day, I&#8217;m quite strict about the scheduling. I have to be up, walk my dog, showered and at the desk by 9:30 at the latest. Then I&#8217;ll have a little lunch break, and in the afternoon when I can&#8217;t write anymore, I&#8217;ll watch a screener or go see a movie. And then I have a big burst of energy around 6, 7, or 8 p.m., and I&#8217;ll move locations, sit at the coffee table, and write there.</span></p><p><strong><span>Hanne:</span></strong><span> You&#8217;re also doing the companion podcast for [the HBO medical drama] The Pitt. That seems like a slightly different muscle set. </span></p><p><strong><span>Hunter:</span></strong><span> Something that I like about the newsletter that I hadn&#8217;t really considered before is that when I write something, everyone knows it&#8217;s coming from Hunter, who&#8217;s a Black woman, who&#8217;s 31, who lives in Brooklyn, who has a dog that&#8217;s a schnoodle but she won&#8217;t say that. She will say that she has a poodle mix, because schnoodle sounds insane.</span></p><p><span>When I was writing for Vulture, writing for a magazine, you don&#8217;t always have that context.
