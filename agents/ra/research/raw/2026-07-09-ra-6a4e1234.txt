										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>The New York Times: It wants to be your one-stop shop for cooking, games, sports&#8230;and next, perhaps, local news?</p>
<p>On Wednesday, the Times <a href="https://www.nytco.com/press/the-new-york-times-to-launch-local-newsletter-in-the-twin-cities">announced</a> it will soon launch The Local, &#8220;a new journalism initiative aimed at serving and engaging communities.&#8221; The national news behemoth will kick off the new initiative in August with a pilot newsletter for the Twin Cities, which will be sent out <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/newsletters/the-local-twin-cities">three times a week</a>, &#8220;with the goal of deepening our relationships with readers in Minneapolis and St. Paul and helping them feel more connected through journalism,&#8221; managing editor <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/marc-lacey-892a358/">Marc Lacey</a> and editorial director of newsletters <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/jodi-rudoren-184a3230/">Jodi Rudoren</a> wrote in the announcement. &#8220;Our hope,&#8221; they added, &#8220;is that The Local: Twin Cities can serve as a model for similar future efforts elsewhere around the country.&#8221;</p>
<p>&#8220;We want to reach people who&#8217;ve never engaged with the Times, deepen our connection with occasional visitors, and serve our most loyal subscribers,&#8221; Rudoren told me in an email. &#8220;We don&#8217;t think what those people want from a local newsletter is all that different: original reporting and analysis, smart curation, reader-focused conversation, clarity, surprise, and delight.&#8221; She emphasized that the newsletter is free to access and will not be limited to Times subscribers.</p>
<p>Per the announcement, &#8220;the voicey newsletter will combine from-the-ground reporting and service with insightful curation of all the Times offers through a local lens.&#8221; The sign-up landing page notes the newsletter will feature &#8220;the latest news, culture, and sports from the Twin Cities.&#8221;</p>
<p>This isn&#8217;t the Times&#8217; first foray into local journalism; most notably, since 2023, its <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/05/the-new-york-times-local-investigations-fellowship-gives-local-reporters-the-time-and-resources-to-take-big-swings/">local investigations fellowship</a> has put its formidable resources at the disposal of local reporters and newsrooms across the country in partnerships that have produced <a href="https://www.pulitzer.org/node/alissa-zhu-nick-thieme-and-jessica-gallagher-baltimore-banner-and-new-york-times">Pulitzer-winning local journalism</a>. But the service journalism emphasis of The Local is an entirely different proposition that suggests The Times wants to step up its efforts to fill the local news void, and/or sees commercial opportunity to build an <a href="https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/posts/2026/06/22/local-news-podcast-holly-moore/">Axios-style local newsletter network</a>. (On Wednesday, Axios Local <a href="https://www.axios.com/press-past-releases/axios-launching-axios-cincinnati-and-axios-north-of-cincy">announced</a> its expansion to its 40th and 41st markets, Cincinnati and the area north of Cincinnati.)</p>
<p>Earlier this year, an in-house ad voiced by A.G. Sulzberger himself <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/03/new-york-times-runs-in-house-ad-asking-listeners-to-support-any-news-organization-dedicated-to-original-reporting/">caught our attention</a> here at Nieman Lab because, rather than ask listeners to subscribe to The New York Times, he called on them to support any news organization &#8220;dedicated to original reporting,&#8221; and noted, &#8220;local newspapers in particular need your support.&#8221; My colleague Josh Benton has argued the Times <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2020/07/an-open-letter-to-the-new-ceo-of-the-new-york-times/">has a responsibility to do more to support local news</a>. &#8220;If the Times is going to be <em>truly</em> mission-driven — if it believes in its &#8216;core journalistic mission of helping people understand the world&#8217; — it should be using its position of relative financial security to work harder on the biggest and most intractable part of the problem,&#8221; he <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2024/02/weve-really-worked-hard-not-to-ever-have-a-pivot-at-the-new-york-times-a-g-sulzberger-on-ai-local-news-and-that-trump-bump/">wrote in 2024</a>.</p>
<p>The Twin Cities are no news desert, as the Times acknowledges. &#8220;We chose the Twin Cities because it&#8217;s a region with a vibrant media ecosystem, hunger for news and tons of creativity,&#8221; Lacey and Rudoren wrote. &#8220;We&#8217;re excited to offer a unique and high-quality newsletter to complement what&#8217;s already there.&#8221; They specify that one of The Local&#8217;s goals is to &#8220;support local news outlets, viewing them not as competitors but as partners.&#8221; Per the announcement, the Times&#8217; newsletter will promote other news outlets&#8217; stories and journalists and &#8220;look for innovative ways to collaborate.&#8221; As examples of the kind of collaboration it anticipates, The Times notes it will record a special episode of the Wirecutter podcast at the Minnesota Public Radio booth at the state fair next month, and will sponsor The Minnesota Star Tribune&#8217;s North Star Summit in September. (Just last month, the Star Tribune <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/06/the-minnesota-star-tribune-will-cut-15-of-its-staff-and-may-become-a-nonprofit/">announced layoffs</a> and said it was exploring becoming a nonprofit.)</p>
<p>The Twin Cities newsletter will be co-hosted by Minnesotans: <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/shadibushra/">Shadi Bushra</a>, formerly of MinnPost, and <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/jaygabler/">Jay Gabler</a>, who joins the project from the Duluth News Tribune. Times Newsletters deputy <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/hannaingber/">Hanna Ingber</a> is leading The Local, and St. Paul native <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/tminsberg/">Talya Minsberg</a> will be the team&#8217;s senior staff editor, &#8220;serving as a crucial link between The Times and [the] journalists in the field, helping make the breadth of our coverage feel urgent and relevant and using video, social media, comments, graphics, data and other storytelling tools to build community.&#8221;</p>
<p>Read more about The Local and the pilot newsletter in the Times&#8217; announcement <a href="https://www.nytco.com/press/the-new-york-times-to-launch-local-newsletter-in-the-twin-cities/">here</a>.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p><span class="dateline">CENTRAL PARK, NEW YORK CITY</span> — On a hot June day in Manhattan, a few minutes away from the sea lion pool in Central Park Zoo, up a hill and through a patch of shoulder-height shrubs, Benji Jones peered into a bottle filled with ethanol and dead bugs.</p>
<p>&#8220;Oh, there&#8217;s some big guys in here,&#8221; he said.</p>
<p>We were standing in front of a Malaise trap, a tent-like structure of cloth and mesh that is in essence a funnel of doom. Flying insects — along with the occasional suicidal and/or unfortunate wingless crawler — are herded into the top of the trap and then into a bottle of ethanol, which quickly kills and preserves them. Every couple of weeks, Jones, a senior environmental correspondent at Vox who focuses on biodiversity (disclosure: Jones is my former colleague, close friend, and a <a href="https://nieman.harvard.edu/class-of-2027/">2027 Nieman Fellow</a>), visits the trap to collect the bugs and swap in new bottles, all in service of a lofty goal: to <a href="https://www.vox.com/climate/489704/new-york-city-new-species-discovery-project">discover a new species</a>.</p>
<div class="nakedboxedimagecaption">
<p><img fetchpriority="high" decoding="async" class="nakedboxedimage" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/Prospect-Park-trap-HQ.jpg" width="700" height="467" /></p>
<p>Benji Jones examines a Malaise trap in Prospect Park.</p>
</div>
<p>New York City may seem like a counterintuitive place to try to find a new creature; it is, after all, a heavily built-up urban environment and not, say, the rainforests of Costa Rica. But that’s kind of the point.</p>
<p>“It&#8217;s an inversion of my usual work, because often I&#8217;m trying to go to these remote places and take stock of the last vestiges of some species that researchers are trying to save,” Jones told me. “Here, we&#8217;re finding new things in the most ecologically disrupted place on Earth. It’s not something that Vox has ever done before. I&#8217;m not sure any newsroom has tried to discover a species like this. Which is exciting, but also makes it a little bit daunting.”</p>
<p>It’s also a reminder that, even in our densest cities, we remain part of an ecosystem. For an insect, New York City — and particularly Central Park and Prospect Park, where Jones set up a second trap — is full of potential habitats. The bugs were there: all Jones had to do was find them.</p>
<p>“It’s an indication of how unexplored our world is,” Jones said. “If you can find a new species in New York City, think how many more there must be out there.”</p>
<p>Back in Central Park, Jones unscrewed the cap on a bottle of fresh, clear ethanol, attached it to the trap along with an excluder — a device to keep large insects like butterflies out — and screwed the cap onto the bottle filled with bugs and brown liquid that had previously occupied its place. This was his second time resetting the traps, which he checks every two weeks.</p>
<p>“I must have gotten a thousand insects in my first round,” he said as he slipped the bottle into a ziplock bag and then into his backpack. “I just have these bottles of bugs sitting in my freezer now.”</p>
<p><div class="storybreak-simple"><span></span></div></p>
<p>Half the work of finding a new species comes down to logistics.</p>
<p>The idea came to Jones when he reported a <a href="https://www.vox.com/down-to-earth/459398/animals-species-unknown-dark-taxa">story on dark taxa</a>, or groups of organisms that are estimated to contain more species than have been discovered or described by scientists. These organisms tend to be small, like insects and crustaceans that we can barely see with the naked eye (although a few years ago I wrote about the discovery of a <a href="https://www.audubon.org/magazine/new-hummingbird-was-discovered-2017-now-theres-race-protect-it">new hummingbird in Ecuador</a>), and often look so similar to other species that the only way to tell them apart easily is by examining their DNA. A <a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2524283123">recent paper in PNAS</a> estimates that there are at least 14 to 20 million insect species on our planet — two to three times higher than previous estimates, and we have discovered only a fraction of them.</p>
<div class="nakedboxedimagecaption">
<p><img decoding="async" class="nakedboxedimage" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/Fergus_13903DSC_7309.jpg" width="500" height="357" /></p>
<p>A fly from the Phoridae family, which Hartop studies. Photo by Steve Marshall, courtesy of Emily Hartop.</p>
</div>
<p>Jones wanted a firsthand look at what it takes to discover a species in 2026. So he reached out to <a href="https://www.ntnu.edu/employees/emily.hartop">Emily Hartop</a>, a taxonomist at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology who studies dark taxa and had conducted a <a href="https://www.newyorker.com/tech/annals-of-technology/los-angeles-back-yard-entomologists">similar study</a> using Malaise traps in Los Angeles that resulted in the discovery of nearly 50 new species more than a decade ago. Hartop connected Jones to <a href="https://www.uoguelph.ca/profile/paul-hebert">Paul Hebert</a>, an evolutionary biologist and founder and CEO of the Center for Biodiversity Genomics at the University of Guelph, in Canada. In 2003, Hebert and his team at Guelph <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1691236/">invented a technique</a> called &#8220;DNA barcoding&#8221; that allows researchers to identify new species using a short sequence of DNA. Every known species on Earth is given an identification number, and if a search through the database doesn&#8217;t turn up a match, it&#8217;s possible the person running the search has found a new species.</p>
<p>&#8220;I know we&#8217;ll find something new,&#8221; Hebert told me when I asked him about Jones&#8217; chances of success (Hartop agrees with his assessment). &#8220;That&#8217;s not a question. We will certainly find many new things.&#8221;</p>
<p>Together with Hartop and Hebert, Jones came up with a plan: He&#8217;d need to apply for a research permit with the New York City Parks Department and talk to the Central Park Conservancy and the Prospect Park Alliance, the nonprofits that manage the two parks, to get permission to set up the traps. Hebert&#8217;s team at Guelph would send Jones the supplies he needed; Jones would collect the samples on a regular schedule and ship them to Canada, where they&#8217;d be barcoded.</p>
<div class="nakedboxedimagecaption">
<p><img decoding="async" class="nakedboxedimage" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/Bottles-HQ.jpg" width="700" height="467" /></p>
<p>Bottles full of insect samples, bagged and ready to be frozen.</p>
</div>
<p>The most likely species to be discovered from this project are small flies and parasitoid wasps, which lay their eggs inside other insects&#8217; bodies. That&#8217;s where the taxonomists come in: after they&#8217;re barcoded, any potential new species will be sent to Hartop, who specializes in flies, as well as another taxonomist who specializes in parasitoid wasps. Jones also wanted to get a research organization based in New York City on board, so he reached out to the American Museum of Natural History. We were joined in Central Park by <a href="https://www.amnh.org/research/staff-directory/sarah-kornbluth">Sarah Kornbluth</a>, a field biologist who&#8217;s an expert on bees.</p>
<p>&#8220;As a journalist, I felt sorely unequipped to put this project together,&#8221; Jones, who dropped out of an ecology Ph.D to pursue journalism, told me. He had to learn how to submit permit requests to the Parks Department, talk to the researchers to understand the sampling protocol, and fill out forms with the US Fish and Wildlife Service so that he could send the samples to Canada. &#8220;Shipping a bunch of dead insects in bags of ethanol is not like shipping a box of chocolates. It&#8217;s much more involved.&#8221;</p>
<p>Jones worked with Vox&#8217;s graphics teams to design signs with the names of the involved organizations as well as QR codes that would take passersby to a <a href="https://www.vox.com/climate/489704/new-york-city-new-species-discovery-project">landing page</a> Vox set up for the project. Signage was an important part of making sure people wouldn&#8217;t disturb Hartop&#8217;s project in LA, and Jones knew his traps in New York, even though they were set back from the trail, would draw many curious eyes.</p>
<div class="nakedboxedimagecaption">
<p><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="nakedboxedimage" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/Sign-HQ.jpg" width="
