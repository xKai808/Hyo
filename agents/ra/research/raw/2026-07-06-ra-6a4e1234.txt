		<title>A quiet shift in how Americans perceive local news</title>
		<link>https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/07/a-quiet-shift-in-how-americans-perceive-local-news/</link>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Local news has long been an exception to a troubling trend. Even as trust in national news organizations eroded over the past decade and <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/10/29/how-americans-trust-in-information-from-news-organizations-and-social-media-sites-has-changed-over-time/">partisan divides over the media widened</a>, local news largely held its ground — <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2024/05/07/views-of-local-news/pj_2024-05-07_local-news-trends_4-03/">majorities of both Republicans and Democrats</a> continued to rate their local news media favorably.</p>
<p>Despite facing <a href="https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2025/report/">major financial challenges</a>, local news <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/06/americans-still-have-faith-in-local-news-but-few-are-willing-to-pay-for-it/">seemed to operate in a different universe</a> than national news — closer to home, less partisan and trusted by a large majority. But <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-news-fact-sheet">new data from the Pew-Knight Initiative</a> shows that may be starting to change.</p>
<p>Whereas 82% of U.S. adults said they trusted information from local news organizations when we first asked this question in 2016, 70% now say the same. The <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/10/29/how-americans-trust-in-information-from-news-organizations-and-social-media-sites-has-changed-over-time/">gap in Republicans’ and Democrats’ trust in local news</a> has also widened slightly, from 6 percentage points in 2016 to 14 points today (figures include those who “lean” toward each party). This is still not nearly as big as the partisan gap in views toward national news. But a space once seemingly insulated from political polarization may be going down a similar path.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/Pew-Research-Center-Trust.png" alt="" width="300" height="auto" class="nakedrightimage" />Recent data also points to shifts in how Americans view the value of local news in society. The share of Americans who say local news outlets are extremely or very important to the well-being of their community has <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-news-fact-sheet/#how-important-is-local-news">dropped 10 points in the past year</a> — from 44% to 34% — with an especially steep decline among Republicans.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/Pew-Research-Center-Importance.png" alt="" width="300" height="auto" class="nakedrightimage" />Taken together, these changes suggest a possible erosion of what seemed to make local news exceptional: its relative immunity to the forces that fractured trust in the national news media. Our data doesn’t offer one single explanation for these trends. But there are several questions worth contemplating.</p>
<p>One is whether changes in Americans’ news consumption habits are impacting their relationship with local news outlets. The modern information environment is highly saturated, with people bombarded through their smartphones at any given moment. About half of Americans, and a large majority of young adults, now say they mostly get news because they happen to come across it, not because they are intentionally seeking it out. And when people <em>are</em> looking for <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/03/24/where-do-americans-turn-first-for-information-about-breaking-news/">breaking news</a>, a declining share — including just 14% of adults under 30 — say they go first to a preferred news organization to find out more.</p>
<p>Indeed, Americans’ ties to specific local outlets appear to be weakening. <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-news-fact-sheet/#sources-of-local-news">Fewer Americans are getting local news from traditional outlets</a> like newspapers and TV stations. More are getting it from online forums like Facebook groups or Nextdoor, or directly from local government officials. And 36% get local news from <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/news-influencers-fact-sheet/#reasons-for-getting-news-from-news-influencers">news influencers</a> — the same portion who get local news from daily newspapers.</p>
<p>This shift is especially pronounced among <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-news-fact-sheet/#local-news-behaviors-and-attitudes-by-demographics">young adults</a>: The most common way adults under 30 prefer to get local news is through social media (41%), beating out news websites and television. And younger adults are less likely than their elders to trust local news, or to follow local news at all. For local news outlets, there is clearly work to be done to gain the attention and trust of the next generation of news consumers.</p>
<p>Another question worth considering is whether the <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/newspapers/#economics">revenue losses</a> that have devastated local newsrooms are starting to impact the public’s experiences with local news. Although more than half of Americans still say their local news outlets are doing well financially, a growing share recognize the struggles: 39% say their local news outlets are not doing well, up from 24% in 2018.</p>
<p>The local news industry <a href="https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2025/report/">has also faced declines in employment</a> and a <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2022/04/05/total-number-of-u-s-statehouse-reporters-rises-but-fewer-are-on-the-beat-full-time/">loss of full-time statehouse reporters</a>. This may be impacting the quality of the local news the industry provides. For example, <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2024/07/24/how-americans-get-local-political-news/">fewer than half</a> of Americans say it’s easy to find the information they need to make voting decisions about local elections.</p>
<p>Americans still tend to regard local news more favorably than they do national news organizations. But that gap appears to be quietly narrowing. The challenge now for local news organizations is to convince the public of their value to their communities — despite daunting financial woes, technological upheaval and growing political divisions.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>&#8220;We are sitting in the middle of a fucking polycrisis.&#8221; That&#8217;s how <a href="https://darrylholliday.com/">Darryl Holliday</a> introduced the first-ever <a href="https://www.newsfutures.org/">News Futures</a> Convention in San Francisco in March. </p>
<p>While the idea of being in crisis is a common theme of U.S. journalism conferences, this was not a traditional journalism conference. News Futures, fiscally sponsored by the nonprofit civic media organization <a href="https://www.citybureau.org/">City Bureau</a>, describes itself as &#8220;a community of practice dedicated to reimagining how communities access and use the information they need to thrive,&#8221; and the 70 or so participants at its convention were there as stewards, funders, or active contributors to News Futures <a href="https://www.newsfutures.org/working-groups">working groups</a>. Many of them have the words &#8220;founder&#8221; or &#8220;co-founder&#8221; in their biographies and have played key roles at civic media organizations like Bureau Local, City Bureau, Conecta Arizona, Documented, El Tímpano, the Listening Post Collective, Outlier Media, and Resolve Philly.  </p>
<p>In the lead-up to the convention, News Futures working groups had been grappling with questions like how to work with civic allies beyond journalism, how to support Spanish-language media ecosystems, and how to develop economic models for <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/02/the-future-of-local-news-is-civic-information-not-declining-legacy-systems-new-report-says">civic information</a>. At the convention, the mandate was to collaboratively develop and vote on campaigns to guide the next round of News Futures initiatives and working groups. While participants acknowledged the direness of the moment, it was almost always referenced as a jumping-off point for action. As journalist and educator <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/vanessa-maria-graber-a46091b6/">Vanessa Maria Graber</a> noted, &#8220;It feels really good to be in community and to have a collective freak-out with our peers, but also figure out the ways that we can support each other and be there for one another.&#8221;</p>
<p>Holliday noted that News Futures has intentionally avoided getting much bigger, so it can remain nimble and avoid competing for funding with members of its network. The small staff, including him, are all part-time contractors. Efforts to scale up are directed toward the growth of signatories to the News Futures Charter — a statement of goals nested under heading such as &#8220;news must evolve,&#8221; &#8220;everyone has a role to play,&#8221; &#8220;storytelling should unite,&#8221; &#8220;collaboration is required,&#8221; and &#8220;a vibrant civic life is our goal.&#8221; At the time of the convention, the charter had more than 700 signatories.</p>
<p>Holliday also wants to grow a broader civic media field that includes participants from a variety of sectors that are &#8220;siblings in this work&#8221; — people working in civic engagement, education, libraries, relational tech, and so on. &#8220;We cannot be in this journalism silo,&#8221; he said.</p>
<p>At the convention, breakout groups developed News Futures &#8220;campaign&#8221; proposals:</p>
<ul>
<li>Presenters from the <strong>shared identity</strong> group talked about engaging News Futures signatories to encourage them to use the charter as a guide for their work.</li>
<li>The <strong>standards of practice</strong> group advocated for developing curriculum, assessments and best practices.</li>
<li>The <strong>shared knowledge</strong> group focused on making research on civic media and its impact more accessible — proposing a possible tool to surface research that already exists (something that the <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/05/heres-a-new-database-for-local-news-research-from-syracuse-university-and-rebuild-local-news/">recently</a> launched <a href="https://www.localnewsresearchhub.com/">Local News Research Hub</a> may accomplish at least in part), and supporting research that fills knowledge gaps.</li>
<li>The <strong>leadership and grassroots support</strong> group talked about supporting, mapping, and networking <a href="https://www.pressforward.news/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/ThisisLocalNewsNow_Web_2025.pdf">information stewards</a> and community builders, and funding local fellows to organize dinners and convene people. The presenters argued that this could be an opportunity to translate the News Futures charter for non-journalists playing critical roles informing their communities.</li>
<li>Finally, the group focusing on <strong>funding and policy</strong> raised the issue of being &#8220;boxed in&#8221; by available revenue sources as defined by the current policy and funding environment. They discussed how they might use people power to swarm policy windows, prototype private capital experiments, and organize allied funders.</li>
</ul>
<p>Participants voted on the proposals they&#8217;d like to see prioritized. The proposal that got the most votes was the one aiming to connect News Futures to informal community networks outside of journalism. In addition to signaling the declining salience of the journalism industry in many people&#8217;s media and information diets, this may also reflect a growing awareness of how community members are helping to inform, organize, and connect their communities. Attendees noted neighborhood efforts like ICE watch signal chats and other community-building and mutual aid efforts. Many were curious about whether and how the civic media field could support or collaborate with community-led information sharing efforts.</p>
<p>&#8220;It would be cool to see some big power moves come out of News Futures in one way or another,&#8221; journalism educator <a href="https://carriebrown.info/">Carrie Brown</a> said. That might include initiatives around media policy, or &#8220;standard setting&#8221; so journalism educators can offer &#8220;a new way of viewing things.&#8221;</p>
<p>Other participants talked about setting standards for information ecoysystems research, and influencing the philanthropic sector. &#8220;My hope is that this group becomes so undeniably interesting to funders that they are seeking the input of members and the group at large,&#8221; <a href="https://jennyeverything.com/">Jennifer Brandel</a> said, &#8220;and taking cues from this group.&#8221;</p>
