Made in partnership with Malcom Gladwell&#8217;s Pushkin Industries, it&#8217;s also a venture he&#8217;s owned from day one. I had seven seasons of a television show at CNN with United Shades of America. That puts me in the showbiz hall of fame. I sort of was aware as it was happening that this was rarefied air that I was in. And then when the show went away, I thought I had plans for my next chapter with another show like that, but the industry sort of turned on those shows. After the murder of George Floyd, there was all this clamor in the industry for &#8220;We need to talk to more Black creators and empower them.&#8221; In one of those conversations, I got my documentary, 1000% Me: Growing Up Mixed. That came out of a conversation with HBO about &#8220;What would you like to work on?&#8221; And actually, the project was their idea. And so I got something out of that clamor. A lot of Black creators got the meetings and got things greenlit but didn&#8217;t actually get projects. By the time it was time to make the projects, the clamor had gone down.</span></p><p><span class="mention-wrap" data-attrs="{&quot;name&quot;:&quot;Ava DuVernay&quot;,&quot;id&quot;:541576,&quot;type&quot;:&quot;user&quot;,&quot;url&quot;:null,&quot;photo_url&quot;:&quot;https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/65184955-7673-43c6-b30f-90ef7bb9c643_1286x1288.png&quot;,&quot;uuid&quot;:&quot;62083d21-1954-4f68-b360-57b510435bb4&quot;}" data-component-name="MentionToDOM"></span><span>, who is one of the most great and acclaimed directors of our time, is right here with me on Substack too. I think one of the reasons she did it is because she&#8217;s like, &#8220;I need to get my own voice out there.&#8221; And she had to independently raise money for her last movie that was based on a New York Times bestselling book. I still have three children and an 89-year-old mom and a wife, and I need to make sure we&#8217;re all eating every day or as often as we want to. So that&#8217;s the big story, and that&#8217;s happening all through the industry. I&#8217;d had all this life in podcasting, as you said, but I never really had what I would define as my own podcast, where I get to do whatever I want to do every week and follow my own nose.</span></p><p><strong><span>What did that mean for booking guests for the show?</span></strong></p><p><span>Most are people that I can reach out to directly. So that, I think, on its face means [the show] is different from most of these quote-unquote celebrity-led podcasts. I&#8217;m really proud of the fact that so far it is people I&#8217;m either in direct contact with or people where I&#8217;m like, &#8220;Oh, I&#8217;ve always wanted to be in direct contact with that person.&#8221; I think about Marc Maron&#8217;s podcast [WTF with Marc Maron], which if you&#8217;re a comedian is the blueprint. It was very clear at some point Marc went from &#8220;These are comedians who I have grudges with that I need to solve&#8221; to &#8220;This is the A-list celebrity whose movie&#8217;s out, and now my podcast is one of the cool stops in town.&#8221; Right? And I don&#8217;t think there&#8217;s anything wrong with that, but I think that I want to at least establish the groundwork of not starting there. I want to start personal.</span></p><p><strong><span>Do you feel like having a built-in subscriber base who already knows your voice gives you the ability to skip the usual swings for A-list guests?</span></strong></p><p><span>I mean, I definitely think that&#8230; like, if Sydney Sweeney called me...</span></p><p><strong><span>Would you say yes?</span></strong></p><p><span>Here&#8217;s what I would say: Do you actually know who I am? It&#8217;s important to me that the people I sit down with have some sense of who I am, because I&#8217;m not an entertainment interviewer, I&#8217;m not a celebrity stopper. Now, my publicist might say differently.</span></p><p><strong><span>Do you have a white whale guest?</span></strong></p><p><span>Yes. But I&#8217;d say I&#8217;m big on not trying to make things happen not in their time.</span></p><p><span>I think there&#8217;s also a need for me to sit down with Denzel Washington at some point. I got invited to his AFI tribute years ago and I met him, and he knew who I was. United Shades of America was such a big deal. If you were of a certain age and at that point a CNN watcher, you saw me at some point. So you never know when someone&#8217;s going to be scrolling on their phone and go, &#8220;Oh, who&#8217;s that guy?&#8221; People are also regularly going, &#8220;Oh, that&#8217;s where you are.&#8221; &#8220;You disappeared. You stopped being on Sunday nights on CNN, and I thought you dropped off the face of the earth.&#8221; So I think it&#8217;s important to stay in some of these social media spaces, because you never know when you&#8217;ll not even find a new audience, but find your old audience.</span></p><p><strong><span>What&#8217;s the clipping strategy for the show?</span></strong></p><p><span>Chris Rock taught me this years ago. He said, &#8220;More people see the trailer than see the movie.&#8221; So I&#8217;m very aware that on some level you want the clips to convert people to listeners of the podcast, but you&#8217;re also aware that a lot of these are just only going to be taken in as content on their own. And so there&#8217;s a little bit of pressure to make sure that it actually works on its own so if they never see the podcast, they got something out of it. Justin reached out to me and said, &#8220;Hey, if you ever want to get into podcasting again, Pushkin might be interested.&#8221; So that&#8217;s why the Pushkin thing started. I, of course, knew of Malcolm Gladwell. I read his books. I &#8220;blinked,&#8221; I &#8220;thunked,&#8221; I did all the things. So then I had a meeting with them. The biggest thing that is important in any deal, especially at this point in my career, is my joke is no new friends, unless those friends really can let you know that they know who you are. So it&#8217;s like I said earlier about Sydney Sweeney wanting to do the podcast. I am not the kind of product&#8212;and I think in showbiz we have to think of ourselves as products sometimes&#8212;that you can just throw me on anything and it&#8217;s going to work. I&#8217;ve met with a bunch of different podcast companies, had some good meetings, but I really kept coming back [to Pushkin].</span></p><p><strong><span>Do you own the show?</span></strong></p><p><span>It&#8217;s funny, I went in going, &#8220;I hope they can pay me for this,&#8221; and then after the way the industry fell apart, I was like, &#8220;Oh, I think I need to own all of it.&#8221; Which means I have to pay for this. I just sort of feel like it&#8217;s important to bet on myself, especially at this point in my career and at this point in the history of this industry, and at this point in the history of this country. Like, I can&#8217;t be giving away pieces of myself knowing where everything is headed. Not to say that I wouldn&#8217;t do that under the right circumstances, but it was just really important to me. Sometimes you accidentally end up owning things because nobody thinks it&#8217;s valuable. And then sometimes&#8212;like the Denzel podcast I didn&#8217;t own, and sometimes I was like, &#8220;It is crazy that I don&#8217;t own that.&#8221; &#8217;Cause nobody else would&#8217;ve come up with that idea.
