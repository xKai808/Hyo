										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>If you have ever lived in the Washington, D.C. metro area, or had the misfortune of being stuck there during a snowstorm, there&#8217;s a good chance you&#8217;ve heard of the <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/weather/2026/05/31/new-chapter-capital-weather-gang/">Capital Weather Gang</a>. For eighteen years they were The Washington Post&#8217;s oracles of the atmosphere, covering local weather on the Post website and on <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/capitalweather.com">social media</a>, developing a dedicated — and incredibly loyal — following along the way.</p>
<p>Over the years, the Capital Weather Gang weathered storms both literal and metaphorical; when the Washington Post <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/02/30-things-the-washington-post-did-wrong-and-19-things-they-could-do-to-fix-it/">laid off</a> <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/04/business/media/washington-post-layoffs.html">more than 300 employees</a>, every member of the Gang kept their jobs. But Jason Samenow, who founded the blog in 2004 before it <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/dc-md-va/2026/06/09/capital-weather-gang-is-entering-new-season/">became a part of the Post in 2008</a>, posted a <a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/washingtondc/comments/1qwxhtn/from_the_team_behind_the_capital_weather_gang/">message</a> in the Washington, D.C. subreddit: &#8220;We&#8217;re still here providing around-the-clock D.C.-area weather coverage across the same platforms many of you already follow. But we&#8217;re always thinking about how to better serve and connect with our audience. More to come&#8230;&#8221;</p>
<p>On the last day of May, it became clear what Samenow had been hinting at. The Capital Weather Gang <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/weather/2026/05/31/new-chapter-capital-weather-gang/">published a post</a> titled &#8220;A new chapter for the Capital Weather Gang,&#8221; which announced the blog was returning to its roots and going independent. It was also dropping the &#8220;Gang&#8221; from its name to become, simply, <a href="https://www.capitalweather.com">Capital Weather</a>.</p>
<p>&#8220;We wanted to expand beyond just being sort of a local weather desk,&#8221; Samenow told me. &#8220;We had this idea of having a media ecosphere, which would include a website, newsletter, app, and maybe eventually consulting services to provide the best available information for people interested in weather in D.C.&#8221;</p>
<p>Capital Weather is going independent at a time when both audiences and the weather look incredibly different from when it was first founded. Smartphones have given people access to weather data in the palms of their hands, and climate change is upending weather patterns around the world. To address the first, Capital Weather <a href="https://www.capitalweather.com/mobile-app/">launched its own app</a> alongside its website on the same day the departure announcement went live on the Washington Post website.</p>
<p>&#8220;Apps from the big companies may have more bells and whistles than us,&#8221; Samenow told me, &#8220;but what they don&#8217;t have is access to the people who live D.C. weather and have 20 years of experience forecasting in D.C.&#8221;</p>
<p>The Post let Samenow and his colleagues — the core, full-time team also includes fellow meteorologists Dan Stillman, Ian Livingston, and Camden Walker — keep the social media accounts they&#8217;d built up at the Post, like the <a href="https://x.com/capitalweather">X account with a million followers</a>, but they had to rebuild their email list (which is &#8220;in the tens of thousands&#8221;) from scratch.</p>
<p>So far, Samenow told me, that has been going well. Capital Weather is entirely self-funded by Samenow and his colleagues and will generate revenue through advertising and <a href="https://www.capitalweather.com/support-us/">subscriptions</a> while it builds up a consulting business (which will be primarily aimed at people who do weather-sensitive work, like snowplow operators). While DC area forecasts and updates will not be paywalled, a $75/year &#8220;Friend&#8221; tier subscription unlocks an in-depth suite of weather and climate tools, as well as an insider newsletter and weekly chats with the team. A subscription at the $150/year &#8220;Founder&#8221; tier, meanwhile, also removes ads, provides access to quarterly video briefings, and promises a free tumbler as well as an invite to a &#8220;Founders gathering.&#8221;</p>
<p>&#8220;We&#8217;ve been surprised by the number of people that have signed up for the higher tier,&#8221; Samenow said, saying a &#8220;significant percentage&#8221; of the &#8220;thousands&#8221; of people who had subscribed in Capital Weather&#8217;s first few weeks were paying for that higher tier, and that &#8220;nearly 10,000&#8221; people had signed up for both the paid and free tiers in less than a month. &#8220;People really want to support us,&#8221; he continued. &#8220;I think we&#8217;ve exceeded our expectations in terms of the revenue we&#8217;ve been able to generate in the first couple of weeks.&#8221;</p>
<p>A glimpse at the comments <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/weather/2026/05/31/new-chapter-capital-weather-gang/">on the page</a> announcing Capital Weather&#8217;s departure from the Post hints at where some of those subscribers are coming from: According to the Post&#8217;s own AI-generated summary, &#8220;The comments express widespread disappointment and frustration over the departure of the Capital Weather Gang (CWG) from The Washington Post, with many readers citing it as their primary reason for subscribing&#8230;Many express intentions to cancel their subscriptions and follow CWG to their new independent platform.&#8221;</p>
<p>Which isn&#8217;t to say that Capital Weather&#8217;s contributors will be disappearing from the Post entirely. While &#8220;pretty much&#8221; everyone who was part of the Capital Weather team came over from the Post, the majority of them are freelance contributors who may continue to write for the Post, especially about national and international weather. Capital Weather is also in discussions with the Post about what an ongoing relationship might look like.</p>
<p>&#8220;People like to get weather from people,&#8221; Samenow told me, and the thing that has always made Capital Weather&#8217;s reporting stand out, &#8220;comes down to the human connection.&#8221; Capital Weather is placing a deep emphasis on dialogue with its audience: meteorologists answer questions and make forecasts, while the audience delivers reports of what they&#8217;re seeing, or what meteorologists call <em>ground truth</em>. &#8220;It&#8217;s a mutually beneficial relationship, because their observations help us tell the weather story,&#8221; Samenow said. &#8220;I think especially now, when AI and bots are taking over the world, that&#8217;s a breath of fresh air for people, and so we&#8217;re trying to lean into that as much as we can.&#8221;</p>
<p>Capital Weather is still using AI for some things, though. &#8220;What I will say about AI is that it does make operating a startup a lot easier,&#8221; Samenow told me. It has helped with data analysis, crunching through large data sets that saved the meteorologists hours in Excel, and has also been used to code some features on the website and app, like a <a href="https://www.capitalweather.com/dc-weather-history/">tool</a> to look up D.C. weather history for every day of the year, which can help readers understand just how much things have changed over the years. Samenow had spent years collecting the data to inform that tool, and the AI helped turn the data into an interactive webpage. &#8220;By using and leveraging AI we were able to design to specification and develop much, much faster than we would have been able to just a few years ago,&#8221; Samenow said.</p>
<p>Climate change is top of mind for Samenow and his team, particularly in the context of extreme weather. The Capital Weather website has a dedicated <a href="https://www.capitalweather.com/climate-room/">Climate Room</a> page, which collects data like historical trends, drought conditions, and carbon dioxide concentrations. That data helps people see how changing weather is a matter of not just seasons but decades.</p>
<p>&#8220;If you&#8217;re writing about weather in 2026, climate change is part of the story, and it&#8217;s your responsibility to provide that context,&#8221; Samenow said. &#8220;There&#8217;s obviously also a public safety mission that we have as a weather reporting team, and we take that seriously.&#8221;</p>
<p>In the future, Samenow said, it&#8217;s possible Capital Weather will expand beyond the D.C. area, perhaps by building out a network or franchising operation. More and more meteorologists — primarily those who used to work on TV — are branching out on their own, Samenow told me: in Alabama, for example, local legend James Spann spun up the <a href="https://alabamaweathernetwork.com/about-us/">Alabama Weather Network</a>, and Samenow has heard from meteorologists around the country who are interested in the Capital Weather model.</p>
<p>But while many of those meteorologists come from the TV world and are naturally inclined to go video-first, the Capital Weather audience, Samenow told me, prefers text to video, at least for quick information. &#8220;There&#8217;s certain moments where video makes sense,&#8221; Samenow said. &#8220;Maybe it&#8217;s a quick explainer of something cool in the sky that you just saw, or a short video update on an important forecast. And then when the weather is really high impact, if you can get on camera and sort of walk people through kind of in a livestream, that experience can be really powerful.&#8221;</p>
<p>It&#8217;s possible those appetites are a reflection of the audience Capital Weather has built over the years: most readers are in their thirties, forties, or fifties.</p>
<p>&#8220;We&#8217;re going to have to think about how to attract some of the younger audiences,&#8221; Samenow said. &#8220;That&#8217;s going to be a challenge, I think. But we did just form <a href="https://www.tiktok.com/@capitalweather?ref=capitalweather.com">a TikTok account</a>.&#8221;</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Over the past year, major news publishers have taken <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/01/news-publishers-limit-internet-archive-access-due-to-ai-scraping-concerns/">aggressive anti-scraping measures</a> to curb the rise of AI crawlers. So far, most of these backend interventions have been out of reach for independent journalists who publish on third-party newsletter platforms.</p>
<p>That is changing with a <a href="https://www.cloudflare.com/press/press-releases/2026/cloudflare-and-beehiiv-introduce-ai-crawl-controls-to-help-independent-publishers-navigate-the-ai-era/">new partnership announced</a> on Tuesday between Beehiiv and Cloudflare, the internet infrastructure company that handles roughly 20% of all web traffic. Now, all Beehiiv creators will have beta access to Cloudflare&#8217;s <a href="https://www.cloudflare.com/ai-crawl-control/">AI Crawl Control services</a> through their dashboards. These tools allow creators to opt in or out of AI agents crawling their work.</p>
<p>Each creator on Beehiv will see granular information about which AI agents are accessing their newsletters, which ones are being blocked, and how much referral traffic is being sent back to them by associated AI products. Creators will also be able to allow or block access for each of those agents. Cloudflare will automatically update these settings to reflect any new agents that hit the web from the same companies.</p>
<p>Among newsletter publishing platforms, this gives Beehiiv creators some of the most on-platform control over how their content is being used by AI products. <a href="https://support.substack.com/hc/en-us/articles/20382615953556-How-can-I-block-AI-from-using-my-Substack-publication-to-train-their-models">Substack offers a setting</a> that disallows scraping via robots.txt files. This signals to AI crawlers that they’re not wanted, but effectively operates on an honor-code system and doesn’t actually block those crawlers. Creators on Ghost have used similar robots.txt file customizations to minimize scraping, <a href="https://forum.ghost.org/t/how-do-you-deal-with-ai-scrapers-on-your-blog/51505" target="_blank">according to user forums</a>.</p>
<p>The Beehiiv partnership is the latest move in Cloudflare’s campaign to give publishers more control over how their work is being ingested by AI models — and to build new business around those controls. Last year, Cloudflare made blocking AI crawlers the default for all of its customers and launched a <a href="https://blog.cloudflare.com/introducing-pay-per-crawl/">“pay-per-crawl” marketplace,</a> which allows Cloudflare customers to charge an AI crawler a small fee each time it accesses its content. Cloudflare is estimated to be <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/05/the-emerging-ai-content-licensing-market-puts-news-publishers-in-a-double-bind-a-new-report-warns/">taking a 30% cut of publisher earnings</a> from the marketplace. (There’s no indication that Beehiiv creators will have direct access to the pay-per-crawl marketplace through the new partnership.)</p>
<p>“As AI changes how people find and consume content, publishers need real leverage,” Tyler Denk, the co-founder and CEO of Beehiiv, said in a statement. “Our partnership with Cloudflare gives creators the data and controls they need to either maximize discovery and distribution, or protect their writing and dictate their own terms.”</p>

But evidence from our newly published <a href="https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026">Digital News Report</a> suggests the demand for news video is growing. In Latin America, the same proportion (47%) say they are regularly watching news videos on Facebook, compared to just 28% in North America.</p>
