										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>For a growing number of local journalists and supporters of local journalism, the relevant question in 2026 is no longer, &#8220;Should public policy to support local news exist?&#8221; but &#8220;What should public policy to support local news look like <em>here</em>?&#8221;</p>
<p><a href="https://www.rebuildlocalnews.org/solutions/state-activity-tracker/">At least 21 states and territories</a> have proposed legislation intended to financially support local news — including government advertising set-asides, employment tax credits, newsroom fellowships, and small business advertising tax credits, among other models. Newsrooms are <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/02/for-the-past-year-illinois-has-been-giving-local-news-outlets-tax-credits-to-support-journalism-jobs-a-new-report-looks-at-how-its-going/">starting to see the money</a> some enacted policies have unlocked. Organizations like <a href="https://www.pressforward.news/press-forward-invests-1-45-million-to-advance-state-policies-supporting-local-news/">Press Forward</a>, <a href="https://news.inn.org/the-next-era-for-nonprofit-news-new-allies-and-long-term-vision/#0">INN</a>, and <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/02/meet-the-first-ever-policy-and-advocacy-director-at-lion-publishers/">LION</a> are all dedicating more funding and resources toward the public policy frontier. And the nonprofit <a href="https://www.rebuildlocalnews.org/">Rebuild Local News</a> is hiring coordinators to coalition-build and advance policies in states with lots of activity like <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/posts/rebuild-local-news_pennsylvania-localnews-journalism-activity-7467289035414179840-YQm3">Pennsylvania</a> and <a href="https://www.rebuildlocalnews.org/careers/careers-california-state-manager/">California</a>.</p>
<p>All of this means many people who care about local journalism have inched, or swung, from considering lobbying antithetical to the duties of reporting to realizing they have a role to play in advocating for policies that could bring local newsrooms the dollars they need to survive.</p>
<p>&#8220;In the local journalism world, we are not super-duper experts at the political process,&#8221; Rebuild&#8217;s director of policy <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/matt-pearce-947537123/">Matt Pearce</a> told me. &#8220;The political arena is a different world from the one that we share with each other at conferences, in our trade publications.&#8221;</p>
<p>Pearce, who joined Rebuild last year after many years as a reporter and labor organizer at the Los Angeles Times, has experienced the steep learning curve of the political arena firsthand. Suspecting some busy Nieman Lab readers might value a crash course, I called Pearce in June to get his 10,000-foot view on some of the most important recent developments in local news policy and what he&#8217;s learned from his work with Rebuild. Our conversation, below, has been edited for length and clarity.</p>
<p>They have [also] seen that other states, like <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2019/07/how-free-press-convinced-new-jersey-to-allocate-2-million-for-rehabilitating-local-news/">New Jersey</a> and <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/02/for-the-past-year-illinois-has-been-giving-local-news-outlets-tax-credits-to-support-journalism-jobs-a-new-report-looks-at-how-its-going/">Illinois</a>, have made early efforts to do positive interventions in local news. [That makes legislators feel like] they&#8217;re not doing something super exotic or controversial. I think that&#8217;s part of what you&#8217;re seeing in this snowballing of political interest to do something. There are [now] proofs of concept out there that help make the case for them conceptually.</p>
<p>I think that&#8217;s very important, just raising expectations for what&#8217;s possible politically for the local news world — because our expectations are really very low after many decades of inertia. The sector broadly was so profitable for so long that the nature of the crisis was different in previous eras.</div></p>
<p>We got some really interesting data points in the <a href="https://www.rebuildlocalnews.org/supporting-local-reporting-in-illinois-early-lessons-and-opportunities/">early data back from Illinois</a> and its Journalist Employment Tax Credit: [More than] half the dollars went downstate, outside of Chicago, and [the funding] was sent out with very little administrative overhead. It&#8217;s a very &#8220;efficient&#8221; public policy to have a big broad geographic distribution, compared to something like philanthropy, [which] sometimes can be <a href="https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2025/report/#startups-and-sustainability">more urban focused</a>.</p>
<p>The other data point in that distribution was that most of the benefiting newsrooms were small. Two-thirds of them had six or fewer local journalists. So effectively it was a very efficient way to shoot dollars to a lot of different types of local news outlets, and to achieve a kind of minimum viable political coalition of all the diverse interests that can gain from this type of policy.</p>
<p>That said, we&#8217;ve made some improvements off of the first versions of the journalist employment subsidy. We now recommend, based on what we learned out of Illinois, that benefits be supercharged for the first five journalists employed at a local news outlet, which will end up shifting even more resources toward smaller publications. We also think it&#8217;s really important to include a new hire incentive.</p>
<p>I think those [changes to the journalist employment subsidy] signal something that policymakers find attractive when they&#8217;re trying to balance interests, because they are hearing from every direction, and all these different media stakeholders, about how well or not well policy works for them. The biggest friction point for this type of policy is that a lot of the very smallest micro producers — like a sole proprietor who uses a lot of freelance — are not fully covered.</p>
<p>It&#8217;ll take a whole suite of different interventions to try to cover the different corners of this space and create a balanced portfolio of public support&#8230;.[At Rebuild], in developing our next round of model policies, we&#8217;re talking internally about [ways to] provide some targeted support for the very, very smallest micro publishers to balance out these other types of policies that are getting some political traction, but may be harder to access for [that group].</div></p>
<p>We don&#8217;t have a policy framework right now in the field as an active bill that directly addresses AI scraping across the gamut. There are conversations about [ideas like] organizing into licensing groups, collective rights management kind of like <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/12/journalisms-songwriter-era/">the music industry</a>, copyright&#8230;So that is all very rich territory for a public policy conversation. The AI scraping conversation is very much an <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/09/in-france-ai-revenue-is-going-directly-to-journalists-could-that-happen-in-the-u-s/">international conversation, too</a>.</div></p>
<p><div class="conl"><strong>CULPEPPER</strong>: Is there anything you haven&#8217;t said about how your own thinking has changed around what&#8217;s possible and necessary in policy, and specifically, did you have any wariness about news and policymaking that you think differently about now?</div></p>
<p><div class="conr"><strong>PEARCE</strong>: I think there&#8217;s been a huge sea change in the way the local news world thinks about public policy.</p>
<p>When I was first starting to do this type of work in 2022, there was way more resistance from the news community around me about concerns about government interference and [retaining independence]. [But] I think the writing is on the wall for a lot of folks: The economic crisis in the local news sector is so urgent that the balance of concerns have tipped toward some kind of intervention. And I think some of the early experiences, like in Illinois, have assuaged me [and show] that it is actually possible to create these policies in a way that respects press freedom and doesn&#8217;t create surface area for the executive branch to come in and reward or punish the press based on their journalism or expression. A lot of policies we&#8217;re working on are explicitly designed to avoid creating opportunities for coercion or capture or retaliation. There&#8217;s still philosophical conversations about what kind of government support is appropriate or inappropriate, and I think they&#8217;re extremely important, but that&#8217;s been something that I think has been reassuring.</p>
<p>The more that I do this stuff, the more that I am humbled by the complexity of interests and the need to just keep making marginal improvements every time we go to the plate. We can&#8217;t rest on our laurels and say, &#8220;We had a good idea. It&#8217;s the only idea that&#8217;s possible, the only framework that&#8217;s good.&#8221; [We have to remain] very sensitive to what we&#8217;re learning from the local news space, what we learn from the data, what we learn from the political process, and just continue to adapt. I think a lot of those little marginal improvements are going to quickly add up to a lot.</p>
<p>That&#8217;s not very sexy. A lot of what I&#8217;m doing is not sexy — in fact, a lot of the most important stuff I&#8217;m doing is completely out of public sight, [like] explaining to a legislative staffer where the fault lines are and the challenges they may run into. But it&#8217;s going to matter a lot.</p>
<p>The thing is, it doesn&#8217;t actually take all that much money to make a pretty significant difference for the beneficiaries. That&#8217;s encouraging — though it reflects, to some extent, how much the sector has shrunk. It doesn&#8217;t take that massive of an investment to make a big difference.</div></p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Neither The Boston Globe nor the internet existed in the 1700s. But this past holiday weekend, in celebration of the Semiquincentennial (try saying that five times fast), the newspaper took a liberty of its own: imagining what its home page <a href="https://apps.bostonglobe.com/2026/07/storylab/250th-anniversary-boston/">might have looked like on July 4, 1776</a>. The entire page is a sepia-toned delight that you should take a few minutes out of your day to go through, but a few standouts include:</p>
<p>A &#8220;Patriot Starting Lineup&#8221; of John Hancock, Samuel Adams, and John Adams.</p>
<p>Transit woes, in the style of the 18th century:</p>
<blockquote><p>How is traversing Boston, not much larger than one mile square, still so maddening? To leave this peninsula by carriage requires crossing Boston Neck, that narrow, swampy strip of land beset by wind and waves. In spring, the water reaches a horse&#8217;s knees. The crowded ferry to Charlestown is not a pleasant alternative. But at least the ferrymaster has restored late-night service to 9 p.m. now that British curfews are ended.</p></blockquote>
<p>A weather report of two days of &#8220;fine weather&#8221; followed by &#8220;lightning very hard.&#8221;</p>
<p>A prescient &#8220;comment&#8221; from &#8220;TartanArmyColonel&#8221;: &#8220;Think you Bostonians enjoy your ale? Wait until you meet a thirsty Scotsman.&#8221;</p>
<p>War reporting from the far reaches of&#8230;Staten Island.</p>
<p>A remembrance for Crispus Attucks, the man of African and Wampanoag descent who was the first person killed in the Boston Massacre.</p>
<p>An essential public health report on the end of a smallpox inoculation ban, with some details that sound eerily familiar: &#8220;Today, some skeptics believe inoculation is as dangerous as the disease itself, despite evidence to the contrary&#8230;The treated may freely travel through Boston. But those who resist inoculation must stay locked inside at home, by order of town selectmen.&#8221;</p>
<p>One headline that will remain forever relevant: &#8220;Concord reliev&#8217;d to be free of Harvard students.&#8221;</p>
<p>And, finally, a correction — easier to fix in 2026 than in 1776:</p>
<blockquote><p>We must correct a most unfortunate error: An earlier version of this story said a British official described John Adams as &#8220;incendiary.&#8221; In fact, the official maligned Samuel Adams. We apologize, with reddened faces.</p></blockquote>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Local news has long been an exception to a troubling trend. Even as trust in national news organizations eroded over the past decade and <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/10/29/how-americans-trust-in-information-from-news-organizations-and-social-media-sites-has-changed-over-time/">partisan divides over the media widened</a>, local news largely held its ground — <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2024/05/07/views-of-local-news/pj_2024-05-07_local-news-trends_4-03/">majorities of both Republicans and Democrats</a> continued to rate their local news media favorably.</p>
<p>Despite facing <a href="https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2025/report/">major financial challenges</a>, local news <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/06/americans-still-have-faith-in-local-news-but-few-are-willing-to-pay-for-it/">seemed to operate in a different universe</a> than national news — closer to home, less partisan and trusted by a large majority. But <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-news-fact-sheet">new data from the Pew-Knight Initiative</a> shows that may be starting to change.</p>
<p>Whereas 82% of U.S. adults said they trusted information from local news organizations when we first asked this question in 2016, 70% now say the same. The <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/10/29/how-americans-trust-in-information-from-news-organizations-and-social-media-sites-has-changed-over-time/">gap in Republicans’ and Democrats’ trust in local news</a> has also widened slightly, from 6 percentage points in 2016 to 14 points today (figures include those who “lean” toward each party). This is still not nearly as big as the partisan gap in views toward national news. But a space once seemingly insulated from political polarization may be going down a similar path.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/Pew-Research-Center-Trust.png" alt="" width="300" height="auto" class="nakedrightimage" />Recent data also points to shifts in how Americans view the value of local news in society. The share of Americans who say local news outlets are extremely or very important to the well-being of their community has <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-news-fact-sheet/#how-important-is-local-news">dropped 10 points in the past year</a>
